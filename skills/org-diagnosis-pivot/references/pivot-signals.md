# Pivot Decision Frame

Grounded in Eric Ries's pivot taxonomy (*The Lean Startup*) plus practical startup-operator judgment on when to pull the trigger.

## What a pivot actually is
Keeping **at least one** of market / core capability / business model while changing the rest. If none of the three survive, that's not a pivot — it's closer to shutdown/exit.

## Signals to check (plug your Phase 1 diagnosis findings in here)
1. **Hitting a growth ceiling** — repeated efforts to speed up keep failing, or cash keeps draining with no traction
2. **A core hypothesis has failed validation** — an assumption you were counting on keeps getting disproven by data or experience
3. **Team capability (including your own) doesn't match the direction**, or there's no real buy-in to the vision
4. **Competition has intensified and there's no real moat/differentiation left** — or the org is structurally incapable of supporting the work (e.g., a single engineer serving a backlog that keeps growing, a hard bottleneck baked into the structure)

If any of these are clearly present, don't default to "just keep pushing" — move to the next step.

## Reasons to seriously consider a pivot
- Strong conviction remains in at least one of market / business model / technology
- Unexpected traction has shown up somewhere you weren't looking
- Multiple independent voices — team, customers, industry contacts, investors — are consistently pointing the same direction
- A genuinely bigger opportunity has been spotted

## Pivot types (useful for naming exactly what's changing)
Zoom-in (one feature becomes the whole product) / Zoom-out (a feature expands into a broader service) / customer-segment pivot / customer-need pivot / platform pivot / business-architecture pivot (e.g. B2B ↔ B2C) / business-model pivot / technology pivot / sales-channel pivot

## Persist vs. pivot
There's no universal right answer — it might just be too early, and the market hasn't caught up yet. This frame's job isn't to make the persist-or-pivot call for you; it's to make clear which signal category the current situation falls into.

**Keep the judgment and the execution separate.** "This situation matches a pivot/exit signal" and "I'm acting on that right now" can be two different things — practical constraints (financial runway being the most common one) can mean you know the answer but delay acting on it, and that's not irrational. When that's the case, it's worth writing it down explicitly as "the judgment is made; only the execution timing is still open" so the judgment doesn't get muddied later.
